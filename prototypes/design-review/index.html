⚠ DESIGN REVIEW — three prototypes with fake data. None of this is the real AltaScraper, and nothing here is decided.

Three ways AltaScraper could work

Same fake accounts, same colours, same data in all three — so what differs is how the app works: navigation, where the account is shown, what the home screen is, how listings, the product page, orders and money are organised, and how errors, loading and confirmations behave.

How to review them

  1. Open a prototype below. Each has a yellow bar at the top.
  2. Press Design notes in the yellow bar: every major part gets a yellow tag (A1, B5, C3…). Click a tag to read why it is designed that way. A list of all parts on the screen appears bottom-right.
  3. Use Simulate in the yellow bar to see failure and loading: choose “Amazon refuses the next action”, then submit or save something; choose “slow loading”, then switch account.
  4. Switch accounts in each prototype (“Oakline Supplies” is an empty account, to see empty states).
  5. In A, try the keyboard: Ctrl+K (jump anywhere), J/K move through listings, Enter open, X tick, Esc close, / filter.

Nothing in these files talks to a server. They are plain files in prototypes/design-review/, outside the app; the real app never serves them.

1 · What each direction means

A · Operations Cockpit

What it means
The app is a control panel for someone who already knows the business. Everything is dense, one keystroke away, and edited in place.
Problem it solves
Too many screens and menus (~43), account/marketplace/date scattered across screens, and slow repeat work (open, close, find the next SKU).
How you work
Set the context bar once (account · marketplace · dates). Open a queue (e.g. “Refused”), move down the table with J/K; the product opens beside the table; fix, next, fix, next. Stage price/stock edits, save once.
Why information is where it is
Context on top because every screen obeys it; figures in columns so rows compare at a glance; the product beside (not over) the table so you never lose your place; results at the bottom edge so they don't cover work.
Main action
The row's state action (“Approve”, “Submit”, “Fix & resubmit”) at the end of each row, and the same button at the top of the product panel.
Secondary
Delete, hold, duplicate, preview — in “More”. Rarely used screens only through Ctrl+K.
Advantages
Fastest for daily volume; least clicking; context can never be ambiguous; one place for every failure (Issues).
Disadvantages
Dense — harder for new staff; relies on learning shortcuts; tables are cramped on a phone.
Suits
You, working alone, doing lots of listings and orders every day.

B · Task-Led Workflow

What it means
The app is organised around the four jobs you do, each broken into steps, and every item tells you its one next step.
Problem it solves
“Where do I do X?”, remembering the next step (finding the new SKU, going back to approve), and the product page having no clear way through (audit UX #5).
How you work
Pick a journey (Create listings). The pipeline shows where work is piling up; click a card's one button; the product page opens on the first thing that is wrong; fix it; the button changes to the next step.
Why information is where it is
Stages are the layout (left to right = the listing's life), so position itself tells you status; the checklist on the left says where to start; one primary button at the bottom always says what it will do or why it can't.
Main action
The single “next step” on each card and at the bottom of the product page.
Secondary
Everything not on the path: raw fields, history, other tools live inside the journey that uses them.
Advantages
Easiest to learn and to hand to staff; no dead ends; errors come with the fix; restricted users see only their journeys.
Disadvantages
Slower for bulk work; a pipeline hides the “all listings” comparison; more screens between a problem and a fix when you know exactly what you want.
Suits
Adding staff (listers, fulfilment), or when the app is used occasionally rather than all day.

C · Exception-First

What it means
The app watches every account and brings you only what needs a decision, most money first. Everything that is fine stays quiet.
Problem it solves
Having to go and look (the morning check is 5th in a menu), problems noticed late, and “is the business OK?” answered in four places.
How you work
Open Today. Read the top finding, press its button — the app locks to that account and shows only the rows involved, with a progress bar. Fix them; the finding disappears; back to Today.
Why information is where it is
Profit first (the one question), then findings ranked by money at risk; the evidence (Amazon's words) on the row and at the top of the product page, because it is why you came.
Main action
The finding's button (“Fix them”, “Ship them”, “Set costs”).
Secondary
Normal rows are folded under “fine, not part of this”; rare screens under “Everything else”; snooze / not a problem.
Advantages
Nothing important is missed; least reading; works across all accounts at once; lowest build cost (existing screens reused).
Disadvantages
Only as good as the checks behind it (some Daily checks cannot run today); browsing and bulk work still need ordinary screens; risk of alert fatigue if findings are noisy.
Suits
Running several accounts, checking the business in short sessions, catching problems early.

3 · What can be mixed

Mix freely (independent of each other): A2 context bar · A1 Ctrl+K palette · A9 staged edits · A8 Issues list · B8 errors-with-the-fix · B9 “explain why the button is disabled” · C3 Today home · C7 money headline + waterfall · C8 mission bar · any Orders piece.

Pick one of (they compete for the same space):

  • Product page: A5 beside the table · B5 own checklist page · C5 overlay with evidence first. (B5's checklist can live INSIDE A5 or C5 as a left column.)
  • Home: A3 queue board · B3 journey cards · C3 Today findings. (A3 and C3 are close relatives: C3 is A3 ranked, explained and across accounts.)
  • Navigation: A1 rail · B1 journeys · C1 Today-first. A1's Ctrl+K works with any of them.
  • Listings list: A4 table · B4 pipeline. (C4 is A4 filtered by a finding.)

Conflicts to know about: B2 (one account at a time) and C2 (all accounts on Today) are different ideas of context — they can coexist only as “Today is all accounts, everything else is one account”, which is what C already does.

4 · Recommendation (mine — you decide)

C's home and flow, with A's context and tables, and C's product page carrying B's checklist.

  1. C3 Today + C8 missions as the way in: it answers “what needs me?” across all accounts, and every finding goes straight to the rows that fix it.
  2. A2 context bar on every other screen (account colour, marketplace, dates once) — the account/marketplace confusion is the most dangerous problem this app has had.
  3. A4 tables + A9 staged edits + A8 Issues list for the full lists and bulk work you do every day.
  4. C5 product page (overlay, evidence first) with B5's checklist as its left column for drafts.
  5. A1 Ctrl+K on top of a short menu, so rare screens stay reachable without 43 items.

Why: it keeps the screens you already know (lowest risk to build and learn), puts the account in view permanently, and replaces “go and look” with “here is what needs you”. The cockpit parts protect your speed on bulk days.

Trade-offs: C depends on the checks behind the findings being trustworthy — several Daily checks cannot run today, so some findings would start as “not known”. A mix has more parts to design consistently than a pure direction. B's pipeline view (seeing where listings pile up) is lost unless kept as a view inside Listings. Staff would get a less guided app than pure B.

5 · Good ideas kept in all three

  • Unknown is never zero — missing cost, profit or ad spend says “not known” in every prototype.
  • Confirmations name the account, seller and marketplace (shared confirmation in all three).
  • Amazon's reply stays visible as evidence until the listing is resubmitted.
  • Spending money / changing Amazon is always a deliberate button with a confirmation.
  • The GTIN exemption is only a tick you make; the brand is always the account's own (CLAUDE.md Rule 1).
  • Account context is always visible — three different ways, one per direction.

6 · What these prototypes cannot show

  • Real volumes: hundreds of listings and orders, long titles, many marketplaces at once.
  • Real timing: Amazon's replies, report waits and rate limits are simulated.
  • Screens not built here: Image Studio, repricer, inventory, returns, PPC, keywords, trackers, settings, users/permissions.
  • Restricted users (lister/viewer): B's “fewer journeys” and permission-hidden actions are described, not built.
  • Phone layouts are only basic; accessibility was not tested with a screen reader.
  • Final colours, type and spacing: deliberately NOT designed yet — all three use today's colours on purpose.
